The lawbook, corrected in the open

November 16, 2026 — by Alexander Sanchez

The public lawbook had been live for less than a day when one of the house’s collaborators — DS, who keeps a rulebook draft of his own — read it closely and scored it: 9 of 10. He also withdrew, in full, an objection he had carried against the demo law: a demo here runs on the owner’s own disk, in his own terminal, and its evidence is the receipts — the three things he had called missing were answered by the environment the law runs in. Two deductions remained, and he named both.

The first fix — Part III, the grants. The book stated the principle — a model can never extend, widen, or renew its own grant — but not the mechanism. The mechanism already existed as a ruling of November 14, 2026; it had simply never been carried into the book. It is carried in now, in both editions: a grant’s scope is two-part — an umbrella activation grant and folder grants, both live or the path does not open, and with no folder granted the session is conversation only. The term is the person’s: 30 minutes by default, a hard stop at expiry, no-limit only as the typed choice. Session grants die with the session. And the grant is receipted at both ends, the receipts carrying the grant’s own identifier.

The second fix — Part IV, the dependency sentence. The internal edition, it turned out, needed no edit: its dependency paragraph already carried the runtime clause. The public edition had lost that clause in compression. It is restored, in the reviewer’s tightened form: Dependencies are installed at setup, so an absence is never a designed state; when one is absent or unreachable at runtime, the failure is named, with its remedy. A public edition that loses a clause the internal edition holds is the exact failure the book’s own preamble — decided, built, and proven, kept separate — exists to prevent.

The order was the book’s own: the founder stamped both fixes, they landed in the internal edition first and the public page after, as Site Update 12 — a commit message that names the process, not just the change: proposed by the reviewer, stamped by the founder, internal first.

The reviewer’s revised count: 9.5 of 10. The last half-point, by his measure, is not writing. A lawbook earns it against a real dispute — and that is time, not text.

The same reviewer then read the whole site, and his review carried errors of its own — a live page his tools failed to reach, reported as unreachable; gaps that were already closed. Those corrections are in the record too, his and the house’s alike. What survived checking was a list of seven real fixes, and they land as Site Update 13, alongside this entry.

A command is what you type. The name is what the project is.

— Alexander
