Why the first guide is about permissions

MathSphere Nexus — the Diary

November 18, 2026 — by Alexander Sanchez

Today this site grows one new room: the MN-Dev Guide — the Universal Workshop, where we publish complete instruction manuals for developers. This Diary — the room that was already here, where I write about what we actually run, and why — gets a new entry to mark it.

The Guide and the Diary are deliberately separate. A guide teaches the topic itself — everything a reader needs, down to the last command, with nothing of my house mixed in. The Diary is the personal layer: our choices, our reasons, our mistakes and corrections. A diary entry can point at a guide. It never replaces one.

So why is Entry No. 1 about something as unglamorous as Unix file permissions?

Because everything we build stands on one promise: generous software, with built-in privacy. The tools we make are local-first. Your work stays on your machine. Nothing leaves. A promise like that is not a slogan you print — it is a hundred small settings you get right, and the smallest of them is who is allowed to read a file.

Here is what we run, and why. On the storage that holds my work, private material is owner-only by default: folders at 700, files at 600 — one person can enter, read, and write, and nobody else on the machine can. Material that is meant to be public is public by choice, set openly, never by accident or by default. That is the whole standard, and it fits in one breath: private by default, public by decision.

The Diary will also be honest about the limits, the way the Guide is. Permissions govern the other accounts on the same machine. They do not stop someone holding the physical drive — that is what encryption is for, and it is a separate layer, with its own decisions still ahead of us. Saying what a protection does not do is part of the protection. People cannot trust a workshop that oversells its own locks.

There is a second reason permissions came first. This project keeps records — receipts for what was done, hashes for what a file is — because memory is kind and paper is patient, but a hash does not forget and does not flatter. Before any of that record-keeping means anything, the records themselves have to sit somewhere private. Permissions are the floor the rest of the house stands on. So the floor went in first, and got checked, and got written down where anyone can read how it was done.

That is Entry No. 1. It will not be the last — this guide is going to grow, entry by numbered entry, each one complete enough to work from alone. And this Diary will keep the other half of the story: not just how the thing works, but what we chose, and why, and what it cost to choose it carefully.

— Alexander
