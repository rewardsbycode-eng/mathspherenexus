The receipts book — lost, found, and verified

November 16, 2026 — by Alexander Sanchez

Wizard Central keeps receipts of everything its bridge does — every grant, every call, every refusal — in one hash-chained book: each entry carries the fingerprint of the one before it, so the record can be checked and cannot quietly change. This morning the house discovered that its own first book was gone, found it again, and proved it whole. The story is worth telling in the open, because the book is the house’s own accountability instrument, and it was very nearly lost by the house itself.

How it was lost. The book lives in the machine’s home folder, outside the program — by design, so that deleting the program can never hurt it. Months ago the Chromebook that carried it was refreshed and everything was downloaded again. The program came back; the home folder did not. The current machine has never held the book, and the test suite’s receipts check has been skipping ever since — truthfully. Nothing announced the loss. The book was simply not there, the way a thing is not there when nobody thinks to look.

The wrong turn, owned. Asked where the book lived, the house’s assistant answered from an old note’s inference — almost certainly the phone — and stated it as fact. The same day’s later entry already held the correction: the founder had checked the phone weeks ago, at the time, and found nothing. The assistant had read the earlier entries and stopped before the correction. That slip is in the house’s deviation record, owned in the open, with the rule it produced: when a note carries an almost certainly, read the rest of that day’s entries before repeating it.

The hunt. The phone’s home: checked again, absent again. A phone backup from November 1: opened, searched — no book; the negative is recorded, because a recorded not there is progress. Then a backup of the Chromebook’s home folder, made on October 31 and kept in the founder’s Proton Drive — and there it was, the whole state folder: settings, grants, tools, and the book itself.

The proof. The recovered book holds 133 records, October 14 to October 31, 2026, its sequence unbroken from 1 to 133 — among them entry 128, the first sandbox receipt the system ever wrote. Its fingerprint: c1caf669d078e95491f9aa788f1fc96dcade36f5d0475ac50ef451b6a98d6ecc. And the chain was not taken on faith: it was recomputed, link by link, from the first entry to the last — chain OK, 133 entries. A hash chain’s whole promise is that a found record does not have to be believed; it can be checked. This one was. The founder keeps the files and the proof in his Proton Drive, and copies stand in the house’s own files.

What it produced. A book that can be lost by a refresh is a design flaw, not bad luck — so this morning, before anything else was stamped, the founder stamped receipts permanence: at setup, the program says in plain words where the book lives and what can hurt it; the user keeps a sealed spare copy in a second home of his own choosing — a mirror, never a second book; and restoring is a first-class act, in which a fresh install asks for the backup, verifies the chain before adopting it, and receipts the adoption itself as the book’s next entry. The keeper of all of it is keepwizard, the fleet’s forty-first wizard, whose job list this design now joins. No back door: a book with no spare, on a wiped home, is gone — and the design says so on day one, not after the fact.

Decided is not built, here either: the safety net is a stamped design, and a user who wipes a home today stands where the founder stood. Its first live case is already named — this recovered chain will be homed by hand on the founder’s main machine, under the same discipline, before the built version exists. The book that records everything else now has a keeper, a spare, and a way home.

A command is what you type. The name is what the project is.

— Alexander
