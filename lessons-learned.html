Lessons Learned

November 16, 2026 — by Alexander Sanchez

This blog is not updated casually. It is updated when the house learns something it did not know — and a breakthrough is not ours alone. If we learned it the hard way, someone else is about to, and the lesson belongs where they can read it. This week the lesson came from our own receipts book: the hash-chained record of everything our bridge has ever done. We lost it, hunted it, found it, and proved it whole. The dated account is in the Diary. What it taught us is here.

1. A record is only as safe as the home it lives in. Our book was never deleted. Its home was refreshed away — a machine reset, everything re-downloaded, the program restored and the data quietly not. Any system that keeps records should be able to answer, in plain words, where the record lives and what can hurt it. If the answer surprises you, that is the first bug.

2. Backups are mirrors, never branches. The book survived because a whole-home backup existed, made on an ordinary day for no particular reason. One book, kept in more than one home, identical in each — the moment a backup becomes a second version, you no longer have a record; you have an argument.

3. Verify; don’t remember. Nobody’s recollection settled where the book was — not the founder’s, not the assistant’s. A recompute did: the chain was rebuilt link by link, and the answer came back chain OK, 133 entries. Memory is a claim. A hash is a check. Build systems whose important claims can be checked, and then actually check them.

4. “Almost certainly” is not a fact. The hunt began with a wrong turn: an old note’s inference — almost certainly the phone — repeated as an answer. The correction already existed, later in the same day’s notes, unread. Every statement in a working record is one of three things — a fact, an inference, or a guess — and the discipline is to say which one you are holding, every time.

5. A recorded “not there” is progress. Two archives were opened and honestly searched before the third held the book. Because each negative was written down, the hunt never circled. In any investigation, a verified absence is a result. Record it like one.

6. Build the safety net before you need it — and name its keeper. The morning the book was found, its permanence was designed and stamped: a sealed spare in a home the user chooses, a restore that verifies before it adopts, and one named component — keepwizard, the keeper — whose job the book’s safety is. A safety net designed during the scare is a lesson; designed before it, it would have been a feature. Either way, it does not exist until somebody owns it by name.

7. Write it down plainly; that is how it becomes real. A month of keeping this site’s diary has taught the house its own pattern: the things that get written down plainly are the things that get built — and the things that stay vague stay vague. That is why a breakthrough goes on this page and not only in the Diary. The Diary is where we record what happened. This page is where what we learned goes out to whoever it can save a morning like ours.

The breakthrough was never really recovering the book. It was learning that a system built to keep records for others must first keep its own — and that the keeping, like everything else here, is a design: stated, stamped, and assigned. Those are the lessons. They were earned; take them for free.

A command is what you type. The name is what the project is.

— Alexander
