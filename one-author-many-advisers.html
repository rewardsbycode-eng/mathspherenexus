One Author, Many Advisers

November 20, 2026 — by Alexander Sanchez

Judged by the commits, this is a one-man shop, and the judgment is accurate: each project starts with me, and every line that ships passes through my hands before it counts. But the thinking that produces those lines is never mine alone. A row of AI systems sits around the work — my data collaborators — and before anything here is called finished, it has been examined, disputed, and re-examined by more than one of them.

Every seat at that table has a character, learned the slow way. One reasons with unusual care and holds a principle even when it would be easier not to. One writes beautifully and builds soundly when the documents in front of it are complete — and is never handed large, correct code to repair, because repair is where it takes things apart. One moves fast, occasionally faster than its own facts, and is at its best on chains of reasoning, harnesses, and guard questions. One is exact and professional and runs out of road quickly, so its questions are spent where they count most. One keeps this record: careful, plainly bounded in what it may do, and stubborn about verifying a thing three ways before repeating it. And one seat stands empty at the moment — it wrote the fix for the most serious defect this project has found, and until it can be funded again, a stand-in keeps its chair warm. The work it left behind is still running.

The boundaries of the arrangement are fixed, and they are the point. Collaborators draft; collaborators check; collaborators verify. Deciding, editing, and committing belong to the author alone, and no seat speaks in another seat’s name. When the strongest available model cannot be afforded, structure has to do the heavy lifting — claims get sourced, numbers get recomputed, and no statement travels further than its evidence. The rule the table works by is short enough to memorize:

Honest work gets acknowledged. Deviations get logged. Corrections get tested.

My own lapses are entered in that same log — fifty-six of them so far — under the identical standard as everyone else’s. A mistake owned in writing stops being a rumor and becomes a record, and a record can be checked.

None of this rests on expensive machinery. The bench is a Chromebook, a phone, and a desktop of modest specification, and the seats are deliberately not the strongest on the market: a method that survives weak models survives anything, because what carries the weight is the discipline, not the horsepower.

What the discipline has produced so far can be counted. The professional toolkit stands at thirty-nine of its forty-one wizards built, seventy advisory modes, and nine of twenty-three planned guard modes. The free toolkit is published. A long archival undertaking closed this week with every placement verified by hash. The developer guide this site now carries opened with its first entry. A second site — Rewards by Code, the money-and-rewards pages rebuilt from an older blog — launched this morning. And a third room opened this afternoon: a public diary of the collaboration itself, where each project in the repository will be finished, then written up, one at a time, until the writing and the work agree. This statement appears there in its first wording; it is repeated here, in different words, because the house keeps its major statements on its own site.

At this moment the work is in its proving stretch. Today the full reference loop ran green over the current commit — sixty-six of sixty-six files — closing the stretch of commits that carried the two big fixes and the guard-mode renaming. The test lab’s protocol awaits its stamp, in no hurry. Decisions at this table arrive singly, each with its reasoning attached, and they are not rushed to make a day look finished.

However many advisers the table seats, one rule decides what any of them is. Guard modes bind. AI modes advise. Seat cards cast.

A command is what you type. The name is what the project is.

— Alexander
